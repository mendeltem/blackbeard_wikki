Aussage

  • Use a linear mixed-effects model with subject-specific random intercepts and random slopes for time; this is the standard for longitudinal neuroimaging and is markedly more powerful than repeated-measures ANOVA or per-subject slope extraction [1].
  • Code time as continuous years since baseline for the atrophy rate, and add a non-linear term (spline or piecewise segments) because post-stroke atrophy is not linear — the first ~3 months are 2-4x faster than later periods [5][6].
  • Missing visits: do not use listwise deletion. Mixed models already give valid inference under MAR; where the missingness is informative, use FIML / multiple imputation / IPW [3]. Expect heavy dropout: attrition to <50% by 36 months is described in comparable stroke cohorts [7].
  • Site and scanner: include site and scanner as covariates in the model as the minimal option, but this does not remove scanner-related variance — site explained >50% of volume variance even with a harmonised protocol [13]. Use longitudinal ComBat for longitudinal data; it is more powerful for detecting change than cross-sectional ComBat and controls type I error better than "scanner as covariate" [11][10][12].
  • Scanner change within a site is a real, measurable bias (significant differences in >=67% of cortical/subcortical regions, ~2-4%, after a 3T upgrade [14]); correct it with a transfer term estimated in controls (or in travelling subjects) and model it as a fixed effect [14][15].
  • Head size: do not divide by ICV. Proportional correction biases results and can reverse the direction of associations; residual/adjusted ICV (or ICV as a covariate) is preferable [16][17].
  • Lesion volume must be a covariate: it is an independent predictor of post-stroke cognition and larger lesions are associated with greater later atrophy [4][8][9].
  • Measurement error sets the floor: longitudinal FreeSurfer improves precision by 15-50% over cross-sectional [18][19]; with 2-year follow-up reliability of individual change is only modest and 2-year designs need ~2.7x more subjects than 4-year designs [19]. Report a smallest detectable change, not just a p-value.
  • Normative modelling (centile/z-deviation from a healthy reference) adds individual-level deviation on top of the group-level LME and captures heterogeneity that case-control averages away [20][21].
  • Power: with ~600 patients, 4 visits and long follow-up you are in a comfortable range for whole-brain and subcortical change, but individual-level claims about small structures (entorhinal, choroid plexus) remain underpowered [18][19][2].

Practical recommendation (as written for our setup)

Model (per structure, per hemisphere where ipsi/contralesional contrast is of interest):

  • Outcome: harmonised regional volume (longitudinal FreeSurfer stream, [18][19]), not divided by ICV.
  • Fixed effects: time in years since stroke (continuous) + a non-linear term (piecewise with break at ~3-6 months, or natural spline); time x group interaction of interest; ICV [16][17]; age, sex; chronic lesion volume [7][8]; WMH volume [8]; scanner identity as a factor [14][15]; cognitive status at 6 months if it is the exposure of interest [6].
  • Random effects: random intercept + random slope for time at the subject level; site as a random effect (used in [8]); scanner as a random effect is an alternative but see the warning in [13] that variance-component modelling explained site effects poorly.
  • Harmonisation: longitudinal ComBat with site/scanner as batch and biological covariates preserved [11]; report the unharmonised model as a sensitivity analysis. Use cross-sectional ComBat only if you cannot use the longitudinal variant [10][12].
  • Missing data: REML is the default (valid under MAR); add MICE/FIML/imputation for missing covariates and pre-specified auxiliary variables [3][4]. Report the visit-specific n and the dropout pattern.
  • Scanner changes: maintain a scanner/metadata log; if a change occurred, acquire a bridging sample and estimate a transfer/corrective term from controls as in [14]; re-run the model with a scanner-change indicator.
  • Reporting: for every structure, publish the within-subject SD/CV and smallest detectable change from a test-retest or literature source [20][21][18]; report the group-level atrophy rate with a CI and, separately, the fraction of individuals whose change exceeds the smallest detectable change.
  • Individual-level layer: add a normative-model z-score at baseline and its change as a secondary outcome [22][23], with explicit acknowledgement of the sample-size/uncertainty limits of normative models [24].
  • Power: run the power analysis for the exact LME with the planned visit-specific n and the observed within-subject correlation, and report sensitivity over a range of assumptions [2].

Trägt nicht

Abstract-level evidence, a literature map rather than a systematic review. Open questions:

  • Which scanner/site effects remain after longitudinal ComBat in our cohort, given that site effects persisted even under a harmonised protocol in [13]? Only measurable with a repeat-scan bridging sample.
  • To what extent is our dropout informative (MNAR) with respect to the volume trajectory, and are IPW or a joint model needed in addition to REML? [3][4] give the methods, not the answer for this cohort.
  • Is a single non-linear time model sufficient, or should the early (<6 months) and late (>36 months) phases be analysed as separate models? [5] motivates the question but does not settle the model choice.
  • How large is the scanner-change correction term for our specific upgrade, and can controls provide it? [14] gives a 3.4% figure for a 1.5T->3T change; this is not transferable without our own bridge data.
  • Does ICV itself drift after a scanner change or after large infarcts, and how should it then be handled [13][17]?
  • Which normative reference model matches our segmentation version and age range, and is its uncertainty acceptable for individual-level claims at ~600 patients [23][24]?
  • Thalamic atrophy specifically: [5][7] show stroke-driven thalamic loss, but the relative contribution of direct thalamic infarction vs secondary degeneration to a "thalamic atrophy rate" is not resolved by the retrieved papers.

Ablesung

[1] Bernal-Rusiel JL, Reuter M, Greve DN, et al., 2013, Spatiotemporal linear mixed effects modeling for the mass-univariate analysis of longitudinal neuroimage data, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2013.05.049, PMID:23702413. Shows: extends linear mixed-effects modelling to mass-univariate longitudinal neuroimaging and reports a dramatic gain in statistical power and repeatability of findings over two alternative methods while controlling the false-positive rate — the methodological basis for the recommended LME.

[2] Harrall KK, Muller KE, Starling AP, et al., 2023, Power and sample size analysis for longitudinal mixed models of health in populations exposed to environmental contaminants: a tutorial, BMC Med Res Methodol, doi:10.1186/s12874-022-01819-y, PMID:36635621. Shows: a practical tutorial showing how to align a power analysis with the planned longitudinal mixed model, which inputs are needed (effect size, SD, within-subject correlation) and how to report sensitivity analyses — the basis for the power recommendation.

[3] Li L, Bayat M, Hayes TB, et al., 2025, Missing data approaches for longitudinal neuroimaging research: Examples from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, Dev Cogn Neurosci, doi:10.1016/j.dcn.2025.101563, PMID:40413902. Shows: listwise deletion is not recommended for longitudinal neuroimaging because it can bias results substantially; multiple imputation, propensity-score weighting and full information maximum likelihood are advocated — the basis for the missing-data recommendation.

[4] Chandrasekaran G, Xie SX, 2024, Improving Regression Analysis with Imputation in a Longitudinal Study of Alzheimer's Disease, J Alzheimers Dis, doi:10.3233/JAD-231047, PMID:38640151. Shows: in 1063 ADNI subjects with mild cognitive impairment, MICE imputation before a linear mixed-effects model gave valid but tighter confidence intervals, i.e. improved efficiency over analysis without imputation, while warning that an invalid imputation approach can compromise the analysis.

[5] Brodtmann A, Khlif MS, Egorova N, et al., 2020, Dynamic Regional Brain Atrophy Rates in the First Year After Ischemic Stroke, Stroke, doi:10.1161/STROKEAHA.120.030256, PMID:32772680. Shows: with a linear mixed-effect model over baseline/3/12 months in 113 stroke and 40 control participants, ipsilesional hemispheric, hippocampal and thalamic atrophy rates were 2-4 times greater in the first 3 months than between 3 and 12 months — evidence that time must be modelled non-linearly.

[6] Brodtmann A, Werden E, Khlif MS, et al., 2021, Neurodegeneration Over 3 Years Following Ischaemic Stroke: Findings From the Cognition and Neocortical Volume After Stroke Study, Front Neurol, doi:10.3389/fneur.2021.754204, PMID:34744989. Shows: 3-month-to-3-year total brain volume loss was 20.3 cm3 (SD 14.8) in 93 stroke survivors versus 14.2 cm3 (SD 13.2) in 39 controls (adjusted difference 7.88 cm3, 95% CI 2.84-12.91, p = 0.002), and was larger in those cognitively impaired at 3 months (adjusted difference 10.42 cm3, 95% CI 3.04-17.80) — a concrete post-stroke atrophy rate and its link to early cognitive impairment.

[7] Aamodt EB, Lydersen S, Alnæs D, et al., 2022, Longitudinal Brain Changes After Stroke and the Association With Cognitive Decline, Front Neurol, doi:10.3389/fneur.2022.856919, PMID:35720079. Shows: in 244 stroke survivors from five hospitals followed to 36 months (145 scanned at 18 months, 72 at 36 months), atrophy was greater ipsilesionally and more so on the right, and greater atrophy and larger stroke lesion volumes were associated with cognitive decline — evidence for lesion volume as covariate and for the attrition pattern.

[8] de Kort FAS, Coenen M, Weaver NA, et al., 2023, White Matter Hyperintensity Volume and Poststroke Cognition: An Individual Patient Data Pooled Analysis of 9 Ischemic Stroke Cohort Studies, Stroke, doi:10.1161/STROKEAHA.123.044297, PMID:37901947. Shows: in 1568 pooled patients, linear mixed models with study site as a random effect found a dose-dependent inverse association of WMH volume with all four cognitive domains independent of acute infarct volume, lacunes and old infarcts — direct precedent for site as a random effect and for lesion/WMH covariates.

[9] Ball EL, Shah M, Ross E, et al., 2023, Predictors of post-stroke cognitive impairment using acute structural MRI neuroimaging: A systematic review and meta-analysis, Int J Stroke, doi:10.1177/17474930221120349, PMID:35924821. Shows: pooling 26 papers (27 populations, N = 13,114) with MRI at stroke onset and cognitive outcome at least 3 months later, cerebral atrophy (OR 2.48, 95% CI 1.15-4.62), microbleeds (OR 1.36, 1.08-1.70) and increasing WMH severity (OR 1.26, 1.06-1.49) were associated with later cognitive outcome.

[10] Fortin JP, Cullen N, Sheline YI, et al., 2018, Harmonization of cortical thickness measurements across scanners and sites, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.11.024, PMID:29155184. Shows: across 11 scanners in two multi-site studies ComBat removed unwanted scanner variability while increasing the power and reproducibility of subsequent analyses, and is applicable to lifespan trajectories — the core cross-sectional harmonisation reference.

[11] Beer JC, Tustison NJ, Cook PA, et al., 2020, Longitudinal ComBat: A method for harmonizing longitudinal multi-scanner imaging data, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.117129, PMID:32640273. Shows: with 663 ADNI participants, longitudinal ComBat was more powerful for detecting longitudinal change than cross-sectional ComBat and controlled the type I error rate better than unharmonised data with scanner as a covariate, while modelling additive and multiplicative scanner effects — the key harmonisation reference for a longitudinal design.

[12] Radua J, Vieta E, Shinohara R, et al., 2020, Increased power by harmonizing structural MRI site differences with the ComBat batch adjustment method in ENIGMA, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.116956, PMID:32470572. Shows: in 33 sites (~6000 subjects) ComBat substantially increased statistical significance versus random-effects meta-analysis and slightly increased it versus mixed-effects mega-analysis, with R functions robust to partially missing regions and transferable between datasets — evidence for the power gain and for practical applicability.

[13] Clark KA, O'Donnell CM, Elliott MA, et al., 2023, Intersite brain MRI volumetric biases persist even in a harmonized multisubject study of multiple sclerosis, J Neuroimaging, doi:10.1111/jon.13147, PMID:37587544. Shows: with scan/rescan data from 11 volunteers at four sites, site explained >50% (expanding to >75% across two vendors) of the variation in most estimated volumes, random-effect model assumptions were violated for most comparisons, and differences persisted despite protocol harmonisation — the cautionary counterweight to pure covariate/variance-component adjustment.

[14] Sinnecker T, Schädelin S, Benkert P, et al., 2022, Brain atrophy measurement over a MRI scanner change in multiple sclerosis, Neuroimage Clin, doi:10.1016/j.nicl.2022.103148, PMID:36007437. Shows: after a 1.5T-to-3T scanner upgrade the mean whole-brain volume change was -4.1% in patients and -3.4% in controls; a fixed corrective term of 3.4% (95% CI 3.13-3.67) estimated from controls removed the association of atrophy measures with scanner type (p = 0.29) in a linear mixed-effects model — the template for handling a scanner change with bridge data.

[15] Medawar E, Thieleking R, Manuilova I, et al., 2021, Estimating the effect of a scanner upgrade on measures of grey matter structure for longitudinal designs, PLoS One, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0239021, PMID:34610020. Shows: comparing 3T Verio and Skyra in 116 healthy adults using the longitudinal FreeSurfer stream, between-scanner ICC was >0.8 but paired tests showed significant differences in at least 67% of regions with 2-4% differences, only slightly reduced by offline gradient-distortion correction, plus systematic differences in contrast-based quality metrics.

[16] Dhamala E, Ooi LQR, Chen J, et al., 2022, Proportional intracranial volume correction differentially biases behavioral predictions across neuroanatomical features, sexes, and development, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2022.119485, PMID:35843514. Shows: proportional ICV correction reduced predictive accuracy for surface area and grey matter volume while increasing it for cortical thickness in 1013 adults and 1823 children, and changed the generalisability of models across sexes and age groups — evidence against treating proportional ICV correction as neutral.

[17] Opfer R, Krüger J, Spies L, et al., 2022, Single-subject analysis of regional brain volumetric measures can be strongly influenced by the method for head size adjustment, Neuroradiology, doi:10.1007/s00234-022-02961-6, PMID:35462574. Shows: in a normative database of 5059 T1w images and 127 MS patients, the residual TIV method removed the TIV association in all regions and reduced between-subject variability more than the proportion method, and gave a larger AUC for thalamic atrophy (0.84 vs 0.79); method differences exceeded one z-score point in 5% of subjects.

[18] Reuter M, Schmansky NJ, Rosas HD, et al., 2012, Within-subject template estimation for unbiased longitudinal image analysis, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.02.084, PMID:22430496. Shows: unbiased within-subject template creation for longitudinal FreeSurfer processing reduces variability and avoids over-regularisation, giving a significant increase in precision and discrimination power that can allow smaller sample sizes or shorter trials — the reason to use the longitudinal processing stream.

[19] Jovicich J, Marizzoni M, Sala-Llonch R, et al., 2013, Brain morphometry reproducibility in multi-center 3T MRI studies: a comparison of cross-sectional and longitudinal segmentations, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2013.05.007, PMID:23668971. Shows: across-session test-retest data from 8 sites in 4 countries and three vendors showed longitudinal FreeSurfer segmentation is systematically more reliable across sites regardless of MRI system differences, with reproducibility errors about half of those of cross-sectional analysis for volume segmentations.

[20] Rusinek H, Bokacheva L, Chen H, et al., 2026, Test-retest reliability of FreeSurfer measures of neurodegeneration, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121920, PMID:41966233. Shows: two 3T scans ~5.5 weeks apart in 100 older adults showed longitudinal processing improved precision by 15-50% (largest gain for entorhinal thickness), large well-defined regions (hippocampus, AD signature) were more reliable than small complex ones (entorhinal cortex, choroid plexus), and image quality (Euler characteristic) was the only factor significantly associated with variability.

[21] Vidal-Piñeiro D, Sørensen Ø, Strømstad M, et al., 2025, Reliability of structural brain change in cognitively healthy adult samples, Imaging Neurosci (Camb), doi:10.1162/imag_a_00547, PMID:40800869. Shows: reliability of individual change is modest at 2-year follow-up; longer follow-up increases reliability substantially while adding observations helps little; 2-year designs need about 2.7x (4-year) and 4.0x (6-year) more individuals for comparable power; subcortical volume is more reliable than cortical measures and the longitudinal FreeSurfer stream clearly outperforms the cross-sectional one.

[22] Marquand AF, Rezek I, Buitelaar J, et al., 2016, Understanding Heterogeneity in Clinical Cohorts Using Normative Models: Beyond Case-Control Studies, Biol Psychiatry, doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2015.12.023, PMID:26927419. Shows: introduces normative modelling to parse heterogeneity in clinical cohorts with individual-level predictions, identifying outliers within a healthy reference distribution and relating the magnitude of deviation to symptomatology — the conceptual basis for adding individual deviation analysis.

[23] Ge R, Yu Y, Qi YX, et al., 2024, Normative modelling of brain morphometry across the lifespan with CentileBrain: algorithm benchmarking and model optimisation, Lancet Digit Health, doi:10.1016/S2589-7500(23)00250-9, PMID:38395541. Shows: benchmarking across 37,407 healthy individuals from 87 datasets showed multivariate fractional polynomial regression with non-linear age terms and linear global-measure covariates to be optimal, accurate across the lifespan and stable longitudinally over 2 years, with performance plateauing beyond about 3000 participants.

[24] Bozek J, Griffanti L, Lau S, et al., 2023, Normative models for neuroimaging markers: Impact of model selection, sample size and evaluation criteria, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2023.119864, PMID:36621581. Shows: simulation of normative models for hippocampal volume across sample sizes from 50 to 50,000 shows that bias decreases only modestly with sample size, that uncertainty of model performance is substantial even at large neuroimaging sample sizes and rises sharply at the ends of the age range, and that flexible models perform better — a caution for individual-level normative claims.

[25] Khlif MS, Werden E, Bird LJ, et al., 2022, Atrophy of Ipsilesional Hippocampal Subfields Vary Over First Year After Ischemic Stroke, J Magn Reson Imaging, doi:10.1002/jmri.28009, PMID:34837426. Shows: in 92 stroke and 39 healthy participants with FreeSurfer subfield segmentation, there was no group difference at 3 months but a significant difference in ipsilesional whole-hippocampal volume reduction between 3 and 12 months (p = 0.027, ipsilesional volume smaller at 12 months p = 0.035), with 1.19% ipsilesional atrophy over 9 months, the hippocampal tail contributing 22.7% and CA1 reduction significant ipsilesionally (p = 0.007) and discriminating better than whole hippocampus (p < 0.01).

[26] Pendlebury ST, Rothwell PM, 2009, Prevalence, incidence, and factors associated with pre-stroke and post-stroke dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Lancet Neurol, doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(09)70236-4, PMID:19782001. Shows: pooling 30 cohorts (7511 patients), pre-stroke dementia prevalence was 9-14%; post-stroke (<1 year) dementia ranged from 7.4% (population-based first-ever stroke excluding pre-stroke dementia) to 41.3% (hospital-based recurrent stroke including it), with cumulative incidence beyond the first year only about 3.0% per year; medial temporal lobe atrophy was strongly associated with pre-stroke dementia.

[27] Manco C, Cortese R, Leoncini M, et al., 2024, Hippocampal atrophy and white matter lesions characteristics can predict evolution to dementia in patients with vascular mild cognitive impairment, J Neurol Sci, doi:10.1016/j.jns.2024.123163, PMID:39128160. Shows: in 110 patients with vascular MCI followed for 2 years, 32 progressed to vascular dementia; converters had lower hippocampal volume (p = 0.015) and higher lesion volumes in specific tracts, hippocampal volume alone did not fully explain progression (p = 0.059) but combined with WMH volume the model was significant (p = 0.035).
