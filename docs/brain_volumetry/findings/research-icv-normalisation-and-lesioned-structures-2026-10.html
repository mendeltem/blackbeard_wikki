Literature: head-size normalisation and handling of structures that overlap a stroke lesion

Aussage

  • Estimate head size once per subject and treat it as time-invariant. Longitudinal change must come from tissue, never from a re-estimated ICV: an ICV that itself shrinks with atrophy cancels the very effect being measured [1, 2].
  • ICV estimation is not neutral. FreeSurfer eTIV over-estimated TIV as atrophy progressed in a longitudinal dementia cohort [1], and both SPM- and FreeSurfer-based ICV showed bias related to sex, atrophy or skull size [2]. With 7 sites sharing one protocol, keep the estimation method fixed and QC ICV per subject across visits rather than trusting a single number.
  • SynthSeg is contrast- and resolution-robust and needs no retraining, which is exactly what a multi-scanner MPRAGE cohort needs [3]. In an independent multi-scanner, multi-software comparison (MS) SynthSeg, SAMSEG and FastSurfer gave good to excellent repeatability for large structures, but between-scanner smallest-detectable-change was still 5-29 % - so scanner change, not software, is the dominant error source [4].
  • Do not use the proportional (tissue/ICV) method as the primary normalisation. It flips the sign of the volume-ICV association, changes the direction of sex differences, and is inconsistent with covariate and residual approaches whenever the brain-behaviour association is not null [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].
  • Prefer the residual method (regress region on ICV in a reference/normative sample, use the residual) or include ICV/TIV as a covariate in the model. Across 2320 UK Biobank regression models, adjustment and residual agreed with each other but disagreed with crude and proportional estimates [7]; the residual method removed the ICV association with no direction switch and improved single-subject z-score detection (thalamus AUC 0.84 vs 0.79) [10].
  • If ICV/TIV is entered as a covariate, it must be a fixed degree of freedom spent once per subject; adding it as a covariate was the best-performing method in a matched UK Biobank design [8].
  • Lesion filling is about registration and boundary stability, not about inventing volumes. Filling MS/WMH lesions with WM-like intensity reduces grey-matter over-estimation and improves tissue-volume accuracy [11, 12], but naive filling with normal-appearing white matter introduces artefacts whose magnitude scales with total lesion volume (r = 0.84) [13].
  • Mirroring the contralateral hemisphere ("virtual brain grafting/transplantation") is a legitimate way to restore landmarks so that FreeSurfer parcellation of the lesioned hemisphere matches the intact one; it was validated with sulcal-area comparison [15]. But the filled voxels are synthetic, and the accepted use is to enable parcellation/registration, not to read the filled voxel values as biology [13, 15].
  • WMH are a systematic confound for grey-matter volumetry. WMH overlap inflates caudate and other GM volumes; after WMH correction caudate volume declined with age and separated patients, which uncorrected volumes failed to do [14].
  • In our setup, compute SynthSeg volumes on raw T1 and on the lesion-filled T1 and report both, but make the primary longitudinal model the raw-T1 volumes with lesion load as a covariate; use the filled images only where filling demonstrably stabilises the pipeline.
  • Mask, do not trust. Cortical-thickness bias from focal lesions is reduced by fully masking lesion voxels from tissue probability maps rather than only from summarisation [16]; infarcted ipsilesional vertices should be identified from stroke masks and excluded before any cortical-thickness analysis [17].
  • Expect genuine, region-specific, ipsilesional-greater atrophy (thalamus, hippocampus), and treat it as a signal of secondary degeneration, not as a segmentation artefact [18, 19, 20].

Practical recommendation (as written for a multisite longitudinal stroke cohort)

  1. Head size: estimate ICV/TIV once per subject from the baseline (or best-QC) scan with a single fixed method; QC it across visits as a subject-level stability check, and never let a per-visit re-estimation feed the longitudinal model (atrophy-sensitive TIV would cancel the effect [1, 2]).
  2. Normalisation in the LMM: use ICV as a covariate, or model the region on the residual scale, both computed against a fixed reference/normative sample; do not use the proportional fraction as the primary outcome [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Report the proportional result only as a sensitivity analysis.
  3. Two volume streams: keep the primary longitudinal stream on raw T1 (SynthSeg 2, --robust --qc, per-structure QC >= 0.65, Euler gate) with infarct volume and WMH volume as covariates. Keep the lesion-filled stream as a robustness/secondary stream, reported alongside - and evaluated per structure, because filling effects are structure- and load-dependent [12, 13, 21].
  4. Lesion filling: for the filled stream, prefer a patch-based/in-painting or grafting approach over naive fill-with-NAWM [11, 13]; validate by checking that the difference between raw and filled volumes does not scale with lesion volume (the artefact signature of [13]).
  5. WMH: because WMH overlap inflates subcortical GM volumes and can flip clinical associations [14], always report caudate/putamen/thalamus volumes with and without WMH handling, and consider WMH volume as a covariate rather than only a filling step.
  6. Cortical thickness: exclude/flag lesioned vertices using a priori stroke masks before analysis, and prefer masking lesion voxels out of the tissue probability maps (fully masked) rather than only out of the summarisation [16, 17].
  7. Ipsilesional vs contralesional: do not merge hemispheres. Model ipsilesional and contralesional volumes separately (or their ratio) and expect an ipsilesional excess that is partly secondary degeneration [18, 19, 20, 24]. Where a structure directly overlaps the lesion, flag/exclude it per subject and structure, and record that in the QC table.
  8. Scanner/harmonisation: cross-scanner smallest detectable change dominates software differences [4], so keep longComBat in the plan, harmonise within scanner/site, and report the lesion-filled/raw sensitivity in the same framework.
  9. Reporting: give head-size-normalised volumes with the adjustment method named explicitly, since "ICV-corrected" is not one method and different methods disagree [7, 9, 22].

Trägt

24 references, each retrieved in the task with the literature tool and checked (PMID matches title, DOI registered at doi.org). Each reference carries one "Shows:" sentence from its abstract.

[1] Pengas G, Pereira JM, Williams GB, et al., 2009, Comparative reliability of total intracranial volume estimation methods and the influence of atrophy in a longitudinal semantic dementia cohort, J Neuroimaging, doi:10.1111/j.1552-6569.2008.00246.x, PMID:18494772. Shows: in a longitudinally imaged semantic-dementia cohort, fully automated FreeSurfer over-estimated TIV as atrophy progressed while a mid-cranial PD-based method was least susceptible, so TIV estimation is not atrophy-invariant - the reason to fix head size per subject in a longitudinal study.

[2] Nordenskjöld R, Malmberg F, Larsson EM, et al., 2013, Intracranial volume estimated with commonly used methods could introduce bias in studies including brain volume measurements, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2013.06.068, PMID:23827332. Shows: comparing SPM and FreeSurfer ICV with reference segmentations in 399 scans of 75-year-olds found both over-estimated ICV, SPM biased by sex and atrophy and FreeSurfer by skull size, and hippocampal normalisation associations changed with the ICV measure - direct evidence that ICV choice biases results.

[3] Billot B, Greve DN, Puonti O, et al., 2023, SynthSeg: Segmentation of brain MRI scans of any contrast and resolution without retraining, Med Image Anal, doi:10.1016/j.media.2023.102789, PMID:36857946. Shows: SynthSeg, trained on synthetic scans with fully randomised contrast and resolution, segments scans across six modalities and ten resolutions without retraining, which is what justifies one model across several scanner models and visits.

[4] van Nederpelt DR, Amiri H, Brouwer I, et al., 2023, Reliability of brain atrophy measurements in multiple sclerosis using MRI: an assessment of six freely available software packages for cross-sectional analyses, Neuroradiology, doi:10.1007/s00234-023-03189-8, PMID:37526657. Shows: across three 3 T scanners and six packages (including SAMSEG, FastSurfer, SynthSeg), large-structure ICC was good to excellent and filled vs non-filled images did not differ clearly, but between-scanner smallest detectable change was 4.83-29.25 %, identifying scanner change as the dominant error source.

[5] Voevodskaya O, Simmons A, Nordenskjöld R, et al., 2014, The effects of intracranial volume adjustment approaches on multiple regional MRI volumes in healthy aging and Alzheimer's disease, Front Aging Neurosci, doi:10.3389/fnagi.2014.00264, PMID:25339897. Shows: raw regional volumes correlate positively with ICV while volume-to-ICV fractions correlate negatively, and only residual correction eliminated the ICV correlation and the sex differences - the core argument against the proportional method.

[6] Sanfilipo MP, Benedict RH, Zivadinov R, et al., 2004, Correction for intracranial volume in analysis of whole brain atrophy in multiple sclerosis: the proportion vs. residual method, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2004.03.037, PMID:15275929. Shows: injecting systematic and random ICV/parenchymal error showed the proportion method shifted normalized means and reduced power under random error, whereas residual-based predicted volumes were largely unaffected and stable in power - early evidence preferring the residual method.

[7] Wang J, Hill-Jarrett T, Buto P, et al., 2024, Comparison of approaches to control for intracranial volume in research on the association of brain volumes with cognitive outcomes, Hum Brain Mapp, doi:10.1002/hbm.26633, PMID:38433682. Shows: across 2320 UK Biobank regression models (N = 41,964), the covariate and residual approaches agreed but disagreed with the crude and proportional approaches, and for the hippocampus the proportional approach even reversed the sign of the cognition association - so "ICV-corrected" is not one method.

[8] Dhamala E, Ooi LQR, Chen J, et al., 2022, Proportional intracranial volume correction differentially biases behavioral predictions across neuroanatomical features, sexes, and development, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2022.119485, PMID:35843514. Shows: proportional ICV correction reduced prediction accuracy from surface area and GM volume but increased it from cortical thickness, and changed cross-sex/cross-age generalisability, demonstrating that the choice of ICV correction has feature- and subgroup-specific effects.

[9] Hu M, Lou Y, Zhu C, et al., 2024, Evaluating the Impact of Intracranial Volume Correction Approaches on the Quantification of Intracranial Structures in MRI: A Systematic Analysis, J Magn Reson Imaging, doi:10.1002/jmri.28974, PMID:37702125. Shows: against a TIV-matched gold standard in 141 volunteers, the residual method had the highest consistency (94.4 %), while unadjusted structures differed significantly between sexes - supporting residual correction.

[10] Opfer R, Krüger J, Spies L, et al., 2022, Single-subject analysis of regional brain volumetric measures can be strongly influenced by the method for head size adjustment, Neuroradiology, doi:10.1007/s00234-022-02961-6, PMID:35462574. Shows: in 5059 normative scans the residual method removed the TIV association and cut between-subject variability more than the proportional method, and gave a higher AUC for detecting thalamic atrophy in MS (0.84 vs 0.79) - the single-subject argument for the residual method.

[11] Prados F, Cardoso MJ, Kanber B, et al., 2016, A multi-time-point modality-agnostic patch-based method for lesion filling in multiple sclerosis, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.06.053, PMID:27377222. Shows: a 5D patch-based non-local-means lesion filling that fills lesions with the most plausible texture introduced fewer artefacts and spurious edges than standard fill-with-nearby-WM methods, notably where lesions abut ventricles or deep grey matter.

[12] Valverde S, Oliver A, Lladó X, 2014, A white matter lesion-filling approach to improve brain tissue volume measurements, Neuroimage Clin, doi:10.1016/j.nicl.2014.08.016, PMID:25379419. Shows: refilling WM lesions with intensities drawn from the slice WM distribution reduced the tissue-volume deviation from the lesion-free ground truth across 1.5 T and 3 T data and two segmentation tools, i.e. lesion filling improves tissue-volume accuracy.

[13] van der Weijden CWJ, Pitombeira MS, Haveman YRA, et al., 2022, The effect of lesion filling on brain network analysis in multiple sclerosis using structural magnetic resonance imaging, Insights Imaging, doi:10.1186/s13244-022-01198-4, PMID:35347460. Shows: artefacts in lesion-filled T1w images correlated with total lesion volume (r = 0.84, p < 0.001) and had a major impact on GM segmentation accuracy, so lesion filling should be applied cautiously at higher lesion loads - the load-dependence warning for our filling step.

[14] Dadar M, Potvin O, Camicioli R, et al., 2021, Beware of white matter hyperintensities causing systematic errors in FreeSurfer gray matter segmentations!, Hum Brain Mapp, doi:10.1002/hbm.25398, PMID:33783933. Shows: in 1077 ADNI participants (4321 time-points) WMH overlapped more with GM structures at higher WMH burden, and without WMH correction caudate volume increased with age and failed to separate AD, which changed to decline and group separation after correction - WMH handling flips clinical associations.

[15] Solodkin A, Hasson U, Siugzdaite R, et al., 2010, Virtual brain transplantation (VBT): a method for accurate image registration and parcellation in large cortical stroke, Arch Ital Biol, PMID:21175010. Shows: filling a large left-hemisphere stroke by transplanting tissue mirrored from the non-stroke hemisphere produced no FreeSurfer parcellation difference for 12 regions between lesioned and non-lesioned hemispheres - validation that mirroring restores landmarks for parcellation.

[16] Brennan D, Schneider ALC, Shinohara RT, et al., 2026, Contusions bias cortical thickness estimates after traumatic brain injury: A TRACK-TBI study, Neuroimage Clin, doi:10.1016/j.nicl.2026.104003, PMID:42114207. Shows: in 86 TRACK-TBI participants, unmasked and summarisation-only masking left lesion-related cross-sectional and longitudinal cortical-thickness bias, while fully masking lesion voxels from tissue probability maps attenuated the cross-sectional bias - a concrete lesion-handling recommendation.

[17] Khlif MS, Egorova-Brumley N, Bird LJ, et al., 2022, Cortical thinning 3 years after ischaemic stroke is associated with cognitive impairment and APOE ε4, Neuroimage Clin, doi:10.1016/j.nicl.2022.103200, PMID:36116165. Shows: in the CANVAS stroke cohort, infarcted ipsilesional vertices were identified from a-priori stroke lesion masks and excluded before mixed-effects cortical-thickness analysis - a concrete example of excluding lesioned cortex in a longitudinal stroke study.

[18] Yassi N, Malpas CB, Campbell BC, et al., 2015, Contralesional thalamic surface atrophy and functional disconnection 3 months after ischemic stroke, Cerebrovasc Dis, doi:10.1159/000381105, PMID:25823493. Shows: in 15 anterior-circulation stroke patients imaged up to 3 months, contralesional thalamic volume fell significantly (-2.1 %, p = 0.047) with declining functional connectivity and atrophy correlated with stroke severity - remote structural and functional change coexist.

[19] Geng J, Gao F, Ramirez J, et al., 2023, Secondary thalamic atrophy related to brain infarction may contribute to post-stroke cognitive impairment, J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis, doi:10.1016/j.jstrokecerebrovasdis.2022.106895, PMID:36495644. Shows: after controlling for head size and global brain atrophy, infarct volume independently correlated with ipsilateral thalamic volume (beta = -0.069, p = 0.024), and left thalamic atrophy correlated with poorer cognition - the template for a head-size- and atrophy-adjusted remote-atrophy analysis.

[20] Aamodt EB, Lydersen S, Alnæs D, et al., 2022, Longitudinal Brain Changes After Stroke and the Association With Cognitive Decline, Front Neurol, doi:10.3389/fneur.2022.856919, PMID:35720079. Shows: in 244 Nor-COAST survivors with FreeSurfer at baseline/18/36 months, cortices and subcortices showed greater ipsi- than contralesional atrophy rate (more prominent right), with medial-temporal atrophy after left strokes and larger lesions after right strokes linked to cognitive decline.

[21] Guo C, Ferreira D, Fink K, et al., 2019, Repeatability and reproducibility of FreeSurfer, FSL-SIENAX and SPM brain volumetric measurements and the effect of lesion filling in multiple sclerosis, Eur Radiol, doi:10.1007/s00330-018-5710-x, PMID:30242503. Shows: repeat scanning of MS patients on three scanners showed within-scanner variability lower than between-scanner, ICV normalisation improved FreeSurfer and SPM reproducibility, and MS lesion filling changed FSL/SPM output but not FreeSurfer - directly relevant to a multi-scanner ICV-normalised volumetry.

[22] Brzezinski-Rittner A, Moqadam R, Zeighami Y, et al., 2026, Intracranial volume: To adjust or not to adjust? It is not a matter of if, but how, Imaging Neurosci (Camb), doi:10.1162/IMAG.a.1235, PMID:42206219. Shows: against a TIV-matched UK Biobank gold standard, adding TIV as a covariate was the best-performing adjustment method, and smoothing of cortical metrics itself biased sex-difference estimates - the most recent head-to-head of ICV adjustment methods.

[23] Radwan AM, Emsell L, Blommaert J, et al., 2021, Virtual brain grafting: Enabling whole brain parcellation in the presence of large lesions, Neuroimage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117731, PMID:33454411. Shows: Virtual Brain Grafting generates a lesion-free T1 so that recon-all parcellation succeeds in heterogeneous focal pathology, with mean Dice 0.894 (unilateral) / 0.903 (bilateral) versus 0.617 without grafting, and quantifies the influence of lesion volume and overlap on labeling accuracy.

[24] Zavaliangos-Petropulu A, Lo B, Donnelly MR, et al., 2022, Chronic Stroke Sensorimotor Impairment Is Related to Smaller Hippocampal Volumes: An ENIGMA Analysis, J Am Heart Assoc, doi:10.1161/JAHA.121.025109, PMID:35574963. Shows: pooling 357 chronic stroke patients across 18 ENIGMA cohorts, sensorimotor impairment was associated with ipsilesional (p = 0.005) but not contralesional hippocampal volume, and hippocampal volume was associated with lesion size - evidence that even non-lesioned, ipsilesional structures carry secondary-degeneration signal and should be analysed per hemisphere.

Trägt nicht

Open questions from the report:

  • Which ICV estimator is most atrophy-stable for a mixed Siemens MPRAGE cohort (eTIV vs SynthSeg-derived vs SAMSEG-derived), given that all automated estimators showed some atrophy- or skull-related bias in the older literature [1, 2]? A within-cohort repeat/scan-rescan check on the same subjects would answer this.
  • Does mirror-based contour filling (our infarct-filling step) preserve parcellation accuracy at the lesion margin specifically, or only globally? The grafting literature [15, 23] reports region-level agreement, but the load-dependent artefact warning [13] has not been tested for mirroring in stroke (mostly tested in MS/glioma).
  • How large is the residual-vs-covariate difference in a stroke cohort with WMH and atrophy, and does the choice interact with longComBat?
  • Best criterion to flag a structure as "lesion-overlapping vs secondary-degenerated": overlap fraction, distance from infarct, or ipsilesional-contralesional asymmetry?
  • Whether WMH filling with normal-appearing WM intensity reproduces the caudate-volume bias of [14] in our pipeline, or whether the WMH-filling step already mitigates it.

Claims rest on abstracts, not full texts, unless the report says otherwise.
