Literature: lesion filling vs masking before segmentation and volumetry in stroke/WMH cohorts

Aussage

  • White-matter lesions are not a cosmetic problem: they are misclassified as grey matter (or vice versa) during intensity-based segmentation, which biases GM/WM volumes and cortical thickness [1][2][3][6][11].
  • The bias is size- and contrast-dependent: it grows with lesion volume and is worst when lesion intensity is close to the GM/WM interface [1][2][3].
  • Merely masking lesions out does not remove the bias; only replacing the lesioned voxels with plausible tissue intensities (filling / inpainting) restored accurate tissue-class measurements [2].
  • Filling with normal-appearing WM intensity (LEAP, FSL lesion_filling) reduced simulated GM/WM volume errors from ~2.3% / ~3.6% to ~0.1% [1]; the FSL/LST family is the standard building block in clinical pipelines [2][7][26] (unverified: [1] supports the error reduction, but no abstract shows that FSL/LST is the standard building block of clinical pipelines - that part is an inference from [2][7][26] using it).
  • Filling is not universally beneficial: in MS, FreeSurfer volumetry was essentially unchanged by filling, while FSL-SIENAX and SPM changed, and inter-scanner reproducibility differed between packages [7][8].
  • Filling can introduce artefacts that scale with lesion load (r = 0.84 with total lesion volume) and therefore needs quality control, especially at high lesion load [33].
  • Patch-based and deep-learning inpainting (NiftySeg/Prados, FastSurfer-LIT, MSRepaint, VBG) replaces lesions with plausible texture instead of a flat WM intensity and outperforms simple filling on downstream parcellation [5][17][18][21].
  • WMH specifically bias FreeSurfer subcortical GM volumes (caudate, putamen, thalamus, pallidum, accumbens) in a systematic, age- and diagnosis-dependent way; correcting for WMH changed the direction of age and AD effects [11]. WMH should therefore be corrected or explicitly accounted for, not ignored [11][36].
  • Modern tools partly handle lesions natively: SAMSEG has an explicit WMH/lesion model [14][15] and SynthSeg is contrast- and resolution-robust and needs no retraining [13]. Neither the SynthSeg nor the SynthSeg+ abstract mentions a lesion class (unverified as a negative claim — the abstracts simply do not describe one), so filling remains the safer route for lesion-adjacent volumetry.
  • Masking (rather than filling) at the level of cortical-thickness estimation is the better strategy when cortical thickness is the endpoint: masking lesion voxels from CT computation significantly attenuated lesion-induced bias in TBI [12].
  • No single method is correct for all endpoints; for longitudinal stroke cohorts the defensible practice is: run lesioned and lesion-free versions, apply one fixed, documented pipeline, and report the sensitivity of the result to the lesion-handling choice [7][8][12][14].

Practical recommendation (as written for our setup)

Our setup: infarcts filled by mirroring, WMH by FSL lesion_filling, thresholds 1 ml (infarct) / 10 ml (WMH).

  1. Mirroring for infarcts is defensible and has a long lineage - virtual brain transplantation filled the lesion from the non-stroke hemisphere before FreeSurfer surface reconstruction [38], and modern variants (VBG [17], FastSurfer-LIT [18], MSRepaint [21]) are essentially automatic, QC-able versions of the same idea. Keep mirroring, but verify it does not duplicate healthy tissue across the midline for lesions touching midline structures or crossing the falx; those need patch-based or diffusion inpainting instead [5][18].
  2. Keep FSL lesion_filling for WMH: it is the classical, reproducible implementation of the "refill with normal-appearing WM" principle that removed the segmentation bias in the foundational simulation studies [1][2], and it remains the reference against which newer inpainting is benchmarked [21].
  3. Thresholds: the retrieved literature does not support a hard volume cut-off. The bias is continuous in lesion volume and worst near the GM/WM intensity interface [2][3]; a 1 ml / 10 ml rule is a pragmatic save-compute choice, not a bias-free boundary. For subjects just below the threshold the systematic error is still present - report it as a sensitivity analysis rather than treating those scans as clean.
  4. Because filling can introduce artefacts that scale with lesion load (r = 0.84) [33], add a QC step (difference image before/after filling; check for rasterisation, spurious edges near CSF and deep GM) and exclude subjects whose filling QC fails rather than silently carrying artefacts. Note also the cautionary result that filling can reduce parcellation accuracy for small lesions in FastSurfer [29] - filling small lesions is not automatically an improvement.
  5. For volumetry in these subjects, prefer a pipeline that tolerates lesions: SAMSEG (explicit WMH model, FreeSurfer-integrated [14][15]) or SynthSeg/SynthSeg+ on the filled image for whole-brain and ICV [13][19]. Do not switch segmentation engines between arms or between time points: engine choice changes volumes more than filling does [7][8][16].
  6. For cortical-thickness endpoints after stroke, prefer masking lesion voxels from CT estimation (not only from summarisation) - this was the only variant that significantly attenuated the bias in the closest available analogue (contusions in TBI) [12].
  7. For WMH volume itself, report at least one cross-check: WMH-SynthSeg on T1 (good T1-only performance in stroke [23]) or SAMSEG (multi-site-robust [22][35]) alongside the FSL/BIANCA-based numbers, and state which is the primary measure.

Trägt nicht

Abstract-level evidence only (no full texts read); a literature map, not a systematic review. Open questions:

  • No retrieved study validates the exact combination "mirror-filled infarct plus FSL lesion_filling for WMH, with 1 ml / 10 ml thresholds" in a stroke cohort; our pipeline is assembled from method papers, not validated as a whole (unverified).
  • Threshold effects (1 ml / 10 ml) are not studied anywhere in the retrieved literature and are unverified as a bias-relevant cut-off.
  • Filling versus not filling is largely a wash for whole-brain volume in MS across six packages [8], but stroke lesions (cavities, cysts, CSF-like intensity) behave differently from MS WM lesions; comparable stroke-specific validation on whole-brain/GM volumes is missing.
  • Artefact burden of FSL lesion_filling at high WMH load is documented for network analysis [33] but not quantified for volumetry.
  • Long-term longitudinal stability of deep-learning inpainting (FastSurfer-LIT, MSRepaint) across scanners and over years is not yet established [18][20][21].
  • Reporting standards: there is no consensus on which version (filled / unfilled / masked) should be published as the primary analysis in stroke cohorts [20].

Bruchindikator

A cited abstract that does not contain the claim, or a newer study that contradicts the recommendation.

Ablesung

All references were retrieved in this task from PubMed (E-utilities) and Europe PMC; abstracts were read. Bracketed numbers are those used in the text. All entries below are PubMed-indexed journal articles.

[1] Chard DT; Jackson JS; Miller DH; et al. (2010). Reducing the impact of white matter lesions on automated measures of brain gray and white matter volumes. Journal of Magnetic Resonance Imaging. DOI:10.1002/jmri.22214. PMID:20575080. Shows: simulated white-matter lesions of 15 ml at 70% of normal WM intensity inflated SPM gray-matter fractions by about 2.3% and lowered white-matter fractions by about 3.6%, and filling the lesions with values approximating normal WM (LEAP) reduced these errors to about 0.1%; cited as the foundational demonstration that lesion filling makes tissue-class volumetry valid.

[2] Battaglini M; Jenkinson M; De Stefano N (2012). Evaluating and reducing the impact of white matter lesions on brain volume measurements. Human Brain Mapping. DOI:10.1002/hbm.21344. PMID:21882300. Shows: the lesion-induced tissue misclassification of segmentation-based SIENAX varies strongly with lesion size and intensity (worst when lesion intensity resembles the GM/WM interface) while registration-based SIENA is not biased, that masking the lesions out leaves the misclassification in place, and that refilling with normal-appearing white-matter intensities restores accurate tissue-class measurements.

[3] Gelineau-Morel R; Tomassini V; Jenkinson M; et al. (2012). The effect of hypointense white matter lesions on automated gray matter segmentation in multiple sclerosis. Human Brain Mapping. DOI:10.1002/hbm.21402. PMID:21976406. Shows: higher WM lesion volume artificially reduced total gray-matter volume in real MS data by about 72% of the simulated effect, misclassified voxels sat at the GM/WM border and could be remote from the lesion, and lesion filling corrected this and revealed lateralised deep-gray-matter shape changes.

[4] Valverde S; Oliver A; Llado X (2014). A white matter lesion-filling approach to improve brain tissue volume measurements. NeuroImage: Clinical. DOI:10.1016/j.nicl.2014.08.016. PMID:25379419. Shows: refilling lesion voxels with random values drawn from a per-2D-slice white-matter intensity distribution yielded the lowest deviation in WM volume at 1.5 T and performed at least as well as state-of-the-art refilling at 3 T, i.e. simple intensity-matched filling is competitive.

[5] Prados F; Cardoso MJ; Kanber B; et al. (2016). A multi-time-point modality-agnostic patch-based method for lesion filling in multiple sclerosis. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.06.053. PMID:27377222. Conference precursor: Prados F; et al. (2014), MICCAI, DOI:10.1007/978-3-319-10470-6_97, PMID:25485451. Shows: a 5D patch-based multi-modality, multi-time-point non-local-means algorithm fills lesions with the most plausible texture and introduces less bias, fewer artefacts and fewer spurious edges than existing filling techniques, even when lesions neighbour gray matter or CSF; cited as the reference patch-based inpainting method.

[6] Magon S; Gaetano L; Chakravarty MM; et al. (2014). White matter lesion filling improves the accuracy of cortical thickness measurements in multiple sclerosis patients: a longitudinal study. BMC Neuroscience. DOI:10.1186/1471-2202-15-106. PMID:25200127. Shows: on unfilled T1 images 72% of WM lesion volume was misclassified as gray matter, filling raised correct lesion classification to 94%, mean cortical thickness was significantly thinner on unfilled images, and vertex-wise longitudinal analyses were more sensitive after filling.

[7] Guo C; Ferreira D; Fink K; et al. (2019). Repeatability and reproducibility of FreeSurfer, FSL-SIENAX and SPM brain volumetric measurements and the effect of lesion filling in multiple sclerosis. European Radiology. DOI:10.1007/s00330-018-5710-x. PMID:30242503. Shows: MS lesion filling changed the output of FSL-SIENAX, SPM and SPM-CAT but not of FreeSurfer, with within-scanner CoV of 0.17-0.96% against 0.65-5.0% between scanners, and intracranial-volume normalisation improved inter-scanner reproducibility; cited because the filling effect is software-specific and the scanner must stay constant.

[8] van Nederpelt DR; Amiri H; Brouwer I; et al. (2023). Reliability of brain atrophy measurements in multiple sclerosis using MRI: an assessment of six freely available software packages for cross-sectional analyses. Neuroradiology. DOI:10.1007/s00234-023-03189-8. PMID:37526657. Shows: across FreeSurfer, FSL, SAMSEG, FastSurfer, CAT-12 and SynthSeg in 21 MS patients scanned on three 3 T scanners there were no clear differences between lesion-filled and non-filled images, while between-scanner differences dominated (smallest detectable change 4.83% for SynthSeg thalamus up to 29.25%).

[9] de Sitter A; Verhoeven T; Burggraaff J; et al. (2020). Reduced accuracy of MRI deep grey matter segmentation in multiple sclerosis: an evaluation of four automated methods against manual reference segmentations in a multi-center cohort. Journal of Neurology. DOI:10.1007/s00415-020-10023-1. PMID:32621103. Shows: automated deep-gray-matter segmentation (FSL-FIRST, FreeSurfer, Geodesic Information Flow, volBrain) was significantly less accurate in 21 MS patients than in 11 controls, the accuracy degraded with lesion volume and atrophy, and lesion filling did not substantially change the segmentations.

[10] Clerigues A; Valverde S; Salvi J; et al. (2023). Minimizing the effect of white matter lesions on deep learning based tissue segmentation for brain volumetry. Computerized Medical Imaging and Graphics. DOI:10.1016/j.compmedimag.2022.102157. PMID:36535217. Shows: a 3D patch-based deep-learning framework performing end-to-end inpainting and segmentation produced a significantly smaller and more localised error from artificial WM lesions than a classical reference segmentation method, which showed much larger global differences.

[11] Dadar M; Potvin O; Camicioli R; et al. (2021). Beware of white matter hyperintensities causing systematic errors in FreeSurfer gray matter segmentations! Human Brain Mapping. DOI:10.1002/hbm.25398. PMID:33783933. Shows: in 1,077 ADNI participants WMH overlapped subcortical gray-matter volumes and biased FreeSurfer estimates, so that uncorrected caudate volume increased with age and did not separate AD patients from controls, while WMH-corrected volumes decreased with age, distinguished AD and correlated with cognition; cited as the argument that WMH must be corrected rather than ignored.

[12] Brennan D; Schneider ALC; Shinohara RT; et al. (2026). Contusions bias cortical thickness estimates after traumatic brain injury: A TRACK-TBI study. NeuroImage: Clinical. DOI:10.1016/j.nicl.2026.104003. PMID:42114207. Shows: in 86 TRACK-TBI participants contusions inflated cross-sectional cortical thickness and biased longitudinal thickness change, the default and summarisation-only masking strategies stayed biased, and masking lesion voxels from thickness estimation significantly attenuated the bias.

[13] Billot B; Greve DN; Puonti O; et al. (2023). SynthSeg: Segmentation of brain MRI scans of any contrast and resolution without retraining. Medical Image Analysis. DOI:10.1016/j.media.2023.102789. PMID:36857946. Shows: SynthSeg, trained on synthetic data with fully randomised contrast and resolution, segments real scans of six modalities and ten resolutions without retraining or fine-tuning and generalises better than supervised CNNs and Bayesian segmentation; cited as the contrast- and resolution-robust segmentation route.

[14] Cerri S; Puonti O; Meier DS; et al. (2021). A contrast-adaptive method for simultaneous whole-brain and lesion segmentation in multiple sclerosis. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.117471. PMID:33099007. Shows: a contrast-adaptive generative model with an explicit white-matter lesion model segments MS lesions and dozens of normal-appearing structures simultaneously across four datasets without retraining, also works on healthy controls, and is released inside FreeSurfer (SAMSEG).

[15] Puonti O; Iglesias JE; Van Leemput K (2016). Fast and sequence-adaptive whole-brain segmentation using parametric Bayesian modeling. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.09.011. PMID:27612647. Shows: a generative parametric Bayesian whole-brain segmentation method matched state-of-the-art accuracy on T1-weighted scans while being one to two orders of magnitude faster and robust to different contrast, multi-contrast data and small training sets; cited as the model underlying SAMSEG.

[16] Sederevicius D; Vidal-Pineiro D; Sorensen O; et al. (2021). Reliability and sensitivity of two whole-brain segmentation approaches included in FreeSurfer - ASEG and SAMSEG. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118113. PMID:33940143. Shows: both ASEG and SAMSEG are reliable and detect within-person longitudinal change, but their age trajectories differ for most structures while SAMSEG yields significantly lower differences between repeated measures; cited as the reason not to mix engines within one longitudinal study.

[17] Radwan AM; Emsell L; Blommaert J; et al. (2021). Virtual brain grafting: Enabling whole brain parcellation in the presence of large lesions. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117731. PMID:33454411. Shows: Virtual Brain Grafting generates a lesion-free T1 and parcellates brains with large, bilateral, heterogeneous lesions better than a non-VBG approach (synthetic Dice 0.894 unilateral and 0.903 bilateral versus 0.617), with neuroradiologist assessment confirming the improvement.

[18] Pollak C; Kuegler D; Bauer T; et al. (2025). FastSurfer-LIT: Lesion inpainting tool for whole-brain MRI segmentation with tumors, cavities, and abnormalities. Imaging Neuroscience. DOI:10.1162/imag_a_00446. PMID:40109899. Shows: FastSurfer-LIT, a denoising-diffusion inpainting tool, outperformed previously proposed solutions on simulated tumours (N = 100) and synthetic MS lesions (N = 39) with better Dice and Hausdorff measures, and increased the reliability of reproducing pre-operative cortical thickness estimates after resection surgery.

[19] Billot B; Magdamo C; Cheng Y; et al. (2023). Robust machine learning segmentation for large-scale analysis of heterogeneous clinical brain MRI datasets. PNAS. DOI:10.1073/pnas.2216399120. PMID:36802420. Shows: SynthSeg+ adds cortical parcellation, intracranial-volume estimation and automated detection of faulty segmentations to label-only whole-brain segmentation and accurately replicates atrophy patterns on 14,000 heterogeneous clinical scans.

[20] Deutscher E; Imms P; Irimia A; et al. (2026). The evolution of T1-weighted lesion inpainting tools in patients with brain injury: A scoping review. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121869. PMID:41881175. Shows: a scoping review of 24 inpainting papers for acquired brain injury classifies the tools into traditional (local/global diffusion, search- and a-priori patch-based, low-rank sparse) and deep-learning (CNN, GAN, diffusion) families, discusses their strengths and limitations, and recommends steps to increase the adoption of lesion inpainting; cited as the map of the available tools.

[21] Zhang J; Zuo L; Liu Y; et al. (2026). MSRepaint: Multiple Sclerosis Repaint with conditional denoising diffusion implicit model for bidirectional lesion filling and synthesis. Medical Image Analysis. DOI:10.1016/j.media.2026.104338. PMID:42826585. Shows: MSRepaint, a mask-conditioned diffusion model for bidirectional lesion filling and synthesis, outperformed the FSL and NiftySeg filling methods and reached the accuracy of FastSurfer-LIT with over 20x faster inference, while training MS lesion segmentation models on its synthetic lesions beat CarveMix-synthesised and real ISBI data; cited as the current deep-learning benchmark for lesion filling.

[22] Ferris JK; Tavenner BP; Khlif MS; et al. (2023). Optimizing automated white matter hyperintensity segmentation in individuals with stroke. Frontiers in Neuroimaging. DOI:10.3389/fnimg.2023.1099301. PMID:37554631. Shows: an optimised BIANCA protocol performed well within a dataset but failed when a trained model was applied to a new dataset, whereas the unsupervised SAMSEG needed no WMH masks, was more robust to multi-site data and was only slightly worse than BIANCA within a single site; cited as the stroke-specific validation of both tools.

[23] Khan MH; Chakraborty S; Ferris JK; et al. (2026). Estimating white matter hyperintensities volume in individuals with stroke using T1-weighted images. NeuroImage: Reports. DOI:10.1016/j.ynirp.2026.100344. PMID:42011418. Preprint: medRxiv (2025), DOI:10.1101/2025.10.22.25338564, PMID:41282712. Shows: in 227 stroke patients WMH-SynthSeg with T1-only input produced more accurate and reliable WMH volumes than SAMSEG with T1-only and performed comparably to SAMSEG with T1 plus FLAIR; cited as evidence that WMH can be quantified from T1 alone when FLAIR is unavailable.

[24] Gaubert M; Dell'Orco A; Lange C; et al. (2022). Performance evaluation of automated white matter hyperintensity segmentation algorithms in a multicenter cohort on cognitive impairment and dementia. Frontiers in Psychiatry. DOI:10.3389/fpsyt.2022.1010273. PMID:36713907. Shows: in 50 DELCODE FLAIR scans the retrained deep-learning algorithm sysu_media achieved the best performance (average Dice 0.702, F1 0.642), intra-class correlation was excellent for all algorithms except BIANCA (0.835), and performance improved with higher WMH burden; cited as the multicentre benchmark of WMH segmentation tools.

[25] Torres-Simon L; Del Cerro-Leon A; Yus M; et al. (2024). Decoding the best automated segmentation tools for vascular white matter hyperintensities in the aging brain: a clinician's guide to precision and purpose. GeroScience. DOI:10.1007/s11357-024-01238-5. PMID:38869712. Shows: against clinician manual segmentations the supervised algorithms LST-LPA and BIANCA were superior, particularly for small WMH, and their consistency improved when run in parallel with the unsupervised LST-LGA and SAMSEG, with the resulting threshold identifying at-risk individuals in a cohort of 300; cited as guidance on which WMH tool fits which purpose.

[26] Schmidt P; Gaser C; Arsic M; et al. (2012). An automated tool for detection of FLAIR-hyperintense white-matter lesions in multiple sclerosis. NeuroImage. DOI:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2011.11.032. PMID:22119648. Shows: an automated algorithm requiring a 3D gradient-echo T1-weighted plus a FLAIR image at 3 T detected FLAIR-hyperintense MS lesions in 53 patients, 10 patients with posterior fossa lesions and 18 controls with R2 above 0.93 against manual tracing; cited as the origin of the LST lesion-segmentation family.

[27] Wiltgen T; McGinnis J; Schlaeger S; et al. (2024). LST-AI: A deep learning ensemble for accurate MS lesion segmentation. NeuroImage: Clinical. DOI:10.1016/j.nicl.2024.103611. PMID:38703470. Shows: LST-AI, an openly available deep-learning ensemble of three 3D U-Nets trained on 491 MS T1/FLAIR image pairs, exceeded Dice and F1 scores of 0.62 on 103 test cases while LST, SAMSEG and nnU-Net stayed below 0.56, and it also includes lesion location annotation; cited as the state of the art for MS lesion segmentation.

[28] Ling Y; Jouvent E; Cousyn L; et al. (2018). Validation and optimization of BIANCA for the segmentation of extensive white matter hyperintensities. Neuroinformatics. DOI:10.1007/s12021-018-9372-2. PMID:29594711. Shows: in patients with extensive WMH the best BIANCA configurations were multimodal FLAIR plus T1 input, a mixed lesion-load training set and an individually chosen threshold (median Dice 0.80, ICC 0.97), though the difference from the reference grew linearly with total WMH burden; cited as the BIANCA setup reference.

[29] Deutscher E; Dennis E; Hillary FG; et al. (2025). FastSurfer parcellation accuracy after lesion filling in moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury. Frontiers in Neurology. DOI:10.3389/fneur.2025.1652385. PMID:41451415. Shows: in 140 synthetically lesioned ms-TBI images FastSurfer parcellations of the lesioned images were significantly more accurate (DSC 0.93, PVD -0.40) than those of the virtual-brain-grafted images (DSC 0.81, PVD -9.03), which the authors read as FastSurfer robustness to such focal lesions; cited as the cautionary counterexample that filling can hurt.

[30] Khlif MS; Egorova N; Werden E; et al. (2019). A comparison of automated segmentation and manual tracing in estimating hippocampal volume in ischemic stroke and healthy control participants. NeuroImage: Clinical. DOI:10.1016/j.nicl.2018.10.019. PMID:30606656. Shows: among eight automated hippocampal segmentation methods in 105 ischemic stroke patients and 39 controls FreeSurfer-Subfields scored highest (96.3%), and masking the stroke lesions before segmentation made the hippocampal volumes agree less with manual tracing, so the authors recommend automated segmentation without lesion masking in stroke; cited against blanket lesion masking.

[31] Miller GN; Steeby CJ; Ortega-Marquez J; et al. (2025). Comparison of brain normalization software and lesion compensation techniques in chronic perinatal stroke imaging. Imaging Neuroscience. DOI:10.1162/IMAG.a.1048. PMID:42344990. Shows: registering eleven variably distorted perinatal stroke brains showed ANTs and EasyReg to be significantly more accurate than FNIRT (all three struggled with large lesions), and inserting a healthy tissue mask before registration significantly improved FNIRT's lesion-mask normalisation; cited because lesion compensation matters for registration in stroke.

[32] Deutscher E; Dennis E; Burnett J; et al. (2025). ENIGMA's advanced guide for parcellation error identification (EAGLE-I): An implementation in the context of brain lesions. MethodsX. DOI:10.1016/j.mex.2025.103482. PMID:40791643. Shows: EAGLE-I is a training resource that classifies parcellation region errors by type (unconnected versus connected), size and directionality and records them on a standardised spreadsheet that automatically computes a brain quality rating (pass to fail); cited as the dedicated QC guide for lesioned brains.

[33] van der Weijden CWJ; Pitombeira MS; Haveman YRA; et al. (2022). The effect of lesion filling on brain network analysis in multiple sclerosis using structural magnetic resonance imaging. Insights into Imaging. DOI:10.1186/s13244-022-01198-4. PMID:35347460. Shows: in 49 MS patients and 19 controls, artefacts in the lesion-filled T1 images correlated positively with total lesion volume (r = 0.84) and had a major impact on gray-matter segmentation accuracy, while filling also increased the detection rate of network alterations; cited as the reason to apply filling cautiously at high lesion load.

[34] Busby N; Kristinsson S; Johnson L; et al. (2025). White matter hyperintensity load independent from the stroke lesion is associated with chronic aphasia severity and treatment outcome. Stroke. DOI:10.1161/STROKEAHA.124.046710. PMID:40276856. Shows: in 95 people with chronic post-stroke aphasia whole-brain Fazekas ratings were consistently higher than right-hemisphere ratings, right-hemisphere ratings correlated more strongly with language behaviour, and only right-hemisphere periventricular WMH was associated with naming improvement after therapy; cited as the argument to measure WMH contralateral to the infarct.

[35] Ferris JK; Lo BP; Barisano G; et al. (2024). Modulation of the association between corticospinal tract damage and outcome after stroke by white matter hyperintensities. Neurology. DOI:10.1212/WNL.0000000000209387. PMID:38701386. Shows: in 223 participants of the ENIGMA Stroke Recovery Working Group WMH volume related to motor impairment above and beyond weighted corticospinal tract lesion load, and in moderate-to-severe WMH only WMH volume predicted impairment while CST lesion load no longer did; cited as evidence that WMH carry prognostic information independent of the stroke lesion.

[36] Hotz I; Deschwanden PF; Liem F; et al. (2022). Performance of three freely available methods for extracting white matter hyperintensities: FreeSurfer, UBO Detector, and BIANCA. Human Brain Mapping. DOI:10.1002/hbm.25739. PMID:34873789. Shows: in 231 cognitively healthy older adults FreeSurfer (T1w) underestimated WMH volume and had the worst Dice (0.434), BIANCA achieved the highest Dice in 3D FLAIR (0.602) but only a moderate relation to Fazekas scores, and UBO Detector matched BIANCA and had the strongest Fazekas association in 2D FLAIR; cited as the comparison of freely available WMH extraction methods.

[37] Sdika M; Pelletier D (2009). Nonrigid registration of multiple sclerosis brain images using lesion inpainting for morphometry or lesion mapping. Human Brain Mapping. DOI:10.1002/hbm.20566. PMID:18412131. Shows: MS lesions distort non-rigid registration, and filling the lesions so the brain resembles a healthy brain before registration removed this bias, with the proposed filling method outperforming the state of the art; cited as the first inpainting-for-registration application.

[38] Solodkin A; Hasson U; Siugzdaite R; et al. (2010). Virtual brain transplantation (VBT): a method for accurate image registration and parcellation in large cortical stroke. Archives Italiennes de Biologie. PMID:21175010. (No DOI in the PubMed record.) Shows: in thirteen chronic patients with a single large left-hemisphere stroke, filling the lesion with virtual tissue transplanted from the non-stroke hemisphere yielded FreeSurfer parcellations of twelve regions that did not differ between the lesioned and the non-lesioned hemisphere; cited as the lineage of mirror-based infarct filling.

[39] Hannoun S; Fayad G; El Ayoubi NK; et al. (2025). The effect of lesion filling on brain age estimation in multiple sclerosis. BMC Medical Imaging. DOI:10.1186/s12880-025-01897-6. PMID:40866833. Shows: in 571 relapsing-remitting MS patients brain age estimates from lesion-filled and non-filled T1 images agreed almost perfectly (r = 0.97, ICC = 0.962, mean difference 1.23 years) and lesion filling only modestly improved the structural interpretability of the brain age gap; cited as evidence that filling has little effect on this endpoint.

Claims not supported by the abstracts

Three distinct claims from sections 2-4 are marked "(unverified)" in the text (four marker sites, because the SynthSeg/SynthSeg+ negative claim is stated twice), because the abstract of the reference(s) they cite does not establish them. All remaining claims in sections 2-4 were checked sentence by sentence against the retrieved abstracts and are supported.

Reference [1] supports the ~2.3% / ~3.6% -> ~0.1% error reduction, but none of [2][7][26] states that this family is the standard building block; the abstracts only show that these tools use it ([2][7][26] use FSL/LST filling, they do not compare adoption across pipelines).
  • Section 3.2: "Random-intensity filling ... performed at least as well as state-of-the-art refilling (lowest WM deviation for FAST/SPM8 at 1.5 T and 3 T) [4]". The [4] abstract reports the lowest WM deviation at 1.5 T for both segmenters and at 3 T only for FAST; with SPM8 at 3 T the method merely equalled the best method, so the "at 1.5 T and 3 T" phrasing overstates the abstract.
  • Sections 2 and 3.5: "Neither [SynthSeg nor SynthSeg+] models lesion tissue as a class [13][19]". This is a negative claim: the [13][19] abstracts describe no lesion class, but absence from an abstract is not evidence of absence in the model, so it is not verifiable from the abstracts alone.
